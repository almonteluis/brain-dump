# Persistence as a scaffolding problem

Kids start a new skill excited. Pencils out, heads down, chatter drops. Then someone hits a wall — fidgeting, looking around, pencil down, done. The motivation was real. The persistence wasn't. The standard read is "they gave up" or "low motivation." But watch the moment: the kid *wanted* to solve it. They just didn't know what to do when confusion arrived. That's not a motivation gap. That's a scaffolding gap.

The question isn't "how do we keep them motivated?" It's "what does the system do in the moment between *confused* and *quit*?" Build a mechanism that rewards persistence — not by giving the answer, but by guiding the next move without solving the puzzle. A hint that's a direction, not a shortcut. The kid still does the thinking; the system just keeps them in the ring another round.

This is the dark side of [[productive-struggle]] — productive struggle assumes the struggle *stays* productive. What happens when it doesn't? The scaffolding IS the bridge between productive and unproductive. And if [[insight-recall]] surfaces past thinking patterns, maybe that's the scaffold — not "here's a hint" but "remember how you figured out that last one? Same energy."
