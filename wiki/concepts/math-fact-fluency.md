# Math Fact Fluency

**Math fact fluency** is the ability to recall basic arithmetic facts (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division) quickly and accurately, typically defined as producing a correct answer within 2–3 seconds. It is considered a foundational skill for higher-order mathematics because fluency frees working memory for complex problem-solving rather than basic computation.

## Definition and Importance

Math fact fluency differs from mere accuracy — it requires **automaticity**, meaning the fact is retrieved from long-term memory without conscious effort. The National Mathematics Advisory Panel (2008) identified fluency with arithmetic facts as a core competency for algebra readiness.

**Why it matters:**
- Students who lack fluency expend working memory on basic computation, leaving fewer cognitive resources for problem-solving, reasoning, and conceptual understanding
- Fluency is a predictor of later math achievement, including algebra performance
- Fluency gaps compound over time — students who don't achieve early fluency fall increasingly behind

## Intervention Evidence

### Retrieval Practice Is the Active Ingredient

[[paper-multiplication-fact-fluency-intervention-modality-2024|Kromminga & Codding (2024)]] conducted an RCT with 72 third graders comparing three modalities:

| Modality | Outcome | Key Finding |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocal peer tutoring | Significant gains | Effective |
| iPad-delivered flashcards | Significant gains | Effective |
| Combined (peer + iPad) | Significant gains | Effective |
| **Between-group difference** | **Not significant** | **Modality doesn't matter; retrieval practice does** |

This is the strongest evidence that the active ingredient is **repeated retrieval practice**, not the social or technological wrapper around it. Schools can choose the delivery method that fits their context.

### Cross-Age Peer Tutoring Is Viable

[[paper-math-fact-fluency-high-schoolers-2024|Powell et al. (2024)]] found that high school students delivered fluency interventions to elementary students as effectively as professional school-based interventionists. Key implications:

- **Scalability** — schools can expand intervention capacity by recruiting and training high schoolers
- **Social motivation** — older peers may provide role-modeling and approachability benefits
- **Intervention integrity** — high schoolers can maintain fidelity with proper training
- **Bidirectional benefit** — peer tutors may develop their own mathematical communication and SRL skills (connects to [[self-regulated-learning]])

## Digital Delivery Validation

Both studies converge on a critical point for ed-tech: **digital fluency tools work**. Kromminga & Codding showed iPad flashcards equal peer tutoring. Powell et al. showed that the intervention itself — regardless of who delivers it — produces measurable gains.

This validates platforms like Teach to One incorporating digital fluency practice:

1. **Core mechanism is retrieval practice** — the digital tool just needs to present facts and require recall
2. **Modality flexibility** — digital can replace or supplement human delivery without efficacy loss
3. **Scalability** — digital delivery removes the human bottleneck
4. **Social layer is optional** — adding peer/social features could boost engagement but isn't required for learning gains

## Persistent Translation Gap

Kromminga & Codding note that evidence-based fluency interventions exist but are often **not implemented in schools**. This translation gap represents both a problem and an opportunity:

- **Problem** — students who need fluency support aren't getting it
- **Opportunity** — digital tools that make fluency practice easy to implement can close this gap
