# Growth Mindset

**Growth mindset** is the belief that intelligence and ability are malleable rather than fixed — that effort and effective strategies can develop one's capabilities. Originally developed by Carol Dweck, it has become one of the most widely cited constructs in education. However, rigorous meta-analytic evidence challenges the effectiveness of growth mindset interventions, while distinguishing mindset from related constructs (mathematical mindset, self-efficacy) reveals that specificity in targeting matters.

## Meta-Analytic Evidence: Near-Zero Effects

[[paper-growth-mindset-meta-analysis-2023|Macnamara & Burgoyne (2023)]] conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis finding that growth mindset intervention effects shrink to near zero when:

- **Active control conditions** are used (vs. no-treatment controls)
- **Publication bias** is corrected for
- **Baseline equivalence** is ensured

No subgroup among 11 preregistered moderators showed a robust, practically significant effect. This sparked controversy (Burnette et al., Tipton et al. commentaries), but the rigorous evidence base is weak.

## How Growth Mindset Actually Works (When It Does)

[[paper-growth-mindset-chinese-math-2023|Dong et al. (2023)]] found that growth mindset does not directly drive achievement — it operates through a chain mediation pathway:

```
Growth Mindset → Self-Efficacy → Intrinsic Motivation → Math Achievement
```

Math anxiety and perceived self-responsibility for failure are additional mediators. This means growth mindset is a distal variable — targeting the proximal mediators (self-efficacy, anxiety) is more likely to produce measurable effects than mindset messaging alone.

## Construct Differentiation

[[paper-growth-mindset-differentiation-2025|Xu & Dieckmann (2025)]] demonstrated that three commonly conflated constructs are empirically distinct:

| Construct | Definition | Pathway to Achievement |
|-----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **Growth mindset** | General belief that intelligence is malleable | Motivational persistence |
| **Mathematical mindset** | Domain-specific beliefs about math ability | Cognitive flexibility |
| **Self-efficacy** | Confidence in performing specific math tasks | Problem-solving strategy use |

Treating these as interchangeable leads to muddled intervention outcomes. Each requires separate measurement and targeted intervention.

## Implications for Teach to One / New Classrooms

1. **Don't build standalone growth mindset features** — The meta-analytic evidence shows generic mindset messaging doesn't move achievement
2. **Target proximal constructs** — Build [[self-determination-theory|competence]] (self-efficacy) and address [[math-anxiety]] directly instead
3. **Measure mathematical mindset separately** — Use domain-specific instruments rather than general mindset scales
4. **Frame for neuroplasticity, not mindset** — Emphasize brain plasticity in math specifically, not generic intelligence malleability
5. **Avoid conflating with self-efficacy** — Track confidence in specific tasks separately from beliefs about ability
