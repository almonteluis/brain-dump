# Transfer of Learning

Transfer of learning is applying what you learned in one context to a new, different context. It's the whole point of education — you don't teach kids fractions so they can do fractions on a worksheet, you teach them fractions so they can reason proportionally about anything. The problem: transfer barely happens.

There are two kinds:

| Type | What it looks like | Does it work? |
|------|-------------------|--------------|
| **Near transfer** | Same structure, different surface features. "I learned to add fractions with circles; now I can add fractions with rectangles." | Yes, reliably. |
| **Far transfer** | Different structure, same underlying principle. "I learned ratio reasoning in math class; now I'm using it to interpret a graph in science class." | Barely. Maybe not at all for most students. |

Perkins and Salomon (1992) said it plainly: near transfer is routine and expected. Far transfer is the exception, not the rule. Most people don't spontaneously see the structural similarity between two problems that look different on the surface — even when the underlying math is identical.

## Why It's the Hard Problem

Every concept in this wiki is, at some level, trying to produce transfer:

- [[master-based learning]] → but mastery in context A doesn't guarantee application in context B
- [[productive-struggle]] → struggle *might* build transferable understanding, or it might just build context-specific strategies
- [[spaced-repetition]] → great for retention, useless for transfer
- [[encoding-specificity-principle]] → actually *works against* transfer, because the more tightly encoded the memory is to its original context, the harder it is to deploy elsewhere

[[insight-recall]] is one of the few approaches that explicitly targets transfer: surface the student's own reasoning in a *new* context, so they have to recognize the structural similarity themselves. It's essentially forcing near-transfer practice and hoping it scales toward far transfer over time.

The open thread: [[project-atlas]] tracks mastery of specific skills on the [[skill-graph]]. But mastery ≠ transfer. A kid who's "mastered" equivalent fractions might not recognize the same equivalence relationship when it shows up in a proportions problem three months later. The gap between "can do" and "can apply elsewhere" is where adaptive systems hit a wall that no amount of data will solve — it's a design problem, not a data problem.
