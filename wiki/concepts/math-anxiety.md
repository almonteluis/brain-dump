# Math Anxiety

**Math anxiety** is a negative emotional response — apprehension, tension, and fear — experienced when engaging with mathematics. It is one of the most well-documented psychological barriers to math achievement and STEM participation. Unlike general test anxiety, math anxiety is domain-specific and can be triggered by numbers, mathematical symbols, or the prospect of performing mathematical tasks.

## Longitudinal Impact on STEM Choices

[[paper-math-anxiety-stem-choices-2024|Cuder et al. (2024)]] — 3-year longitudinal study (middle school → upper secondary):

- Math anxiety **pushes students away** from STEM pathways
- Self-efficacy **pulls students toward** STEM
- The anxiety–efficacy interplay determines STEM trajectory, not achievement alone
- Math anxiety leads to task avoidance, less effective study strategies, and reduced STEM interest

**Critical finding**: Middle school is the intervention window. Motivational factors at this stage have lasting effects on career decisions.

## Relationship to Self-Efficacy

Math anxiety and self-efficacy are partially independent constructs:

- **High anxiety + low efficacy** → most likely to avoid STEM
- **Low anxiety + high efficacy** → most likely to pursue STEM
- **High anxiety + high efficacy** → anxiety can be overcome by confidence
- **Low anxiety + low efficacy** → may drift away from STEM passively

Both constructs must be addressed — reducing anxiety alone is insufficient if self-efficacy isn't built.

## Mechanisms

[[paper-growth-mindset-chinese-math-2023|Dong et al. (2023)]] identified math anxiety as a mediator in the growth mindset → achievement pathway, alongside self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation. [[paper-achievement-goal-profiles-math-2023|Chan & Liem (2023)]] found that math anxiety differentiates between achievement goal profiles — performance-oriented profiles tend to show higher anxiety.

## Reduction Through SDT

[[paper-sdt-math-outcomes-2026|Santiago et al. (2026)]] demonstrated that satisfying [[self-determination-theory|SDT basic needs]] (autonomy, competence, relatedness) in secondary school math classes leads to lower math anxiety in college. This provides a concrete intervention pathway: design learning experiences that build competence, offer choice, and foster belonging.

## Implications for Teach to One / New Classrooms

1. **Measure math anxiety directly** — Don't infer it from performance data. Consider brief anxiety screening instruments
2. **Build self-efficacy as anxiety antidote** — Early successes, scaffolded practice, and competence-supportive feedback
3. **Design anxiety-reducing feedback** — Frame mistakes as learning opportunities, normalize difficulty, avoid time pressure
4. **Address anxiety separately from achievement** — A student can perform well but still be anxious; both need attention
5. **Leverage SDT principles** — Autonomy, competence, and relatedness support all reduce anxiety
6. **Track anxiety over time** — Anxiety should decrease as students progress; if it doesn't, intervention is needed

## CBL Mastery Orientation as Protective Factor

CBL's structural emphasis on mastery-gated advancement creates a **mastery orientation** that is inherently protective against the performance-goal anxiety that Chan & Liem (2023) identified. In a competency-based system:

- **Mastery is the only path forward** — students cannot advance without demonstrating understanding, so the system reinforces mastery goals rather than performance goals
- **Time pressure is removed** — flexible pacing eliminates the anxiety-amplifying effect of timed tests and fixed schedules
- **Revision is expected** — the ability to redo and revise (as Competency Collaborative 2023 documents) normalizes imperfect first attempts
- **Progress is relative to self** — students are measured against their own mastery trajectory, not ranked against peers

This contrasts with traditional grading where performance goals (looking smart, avoiding failure) dominate and anxiety is highest. See [[culturally-responsive-pedagogy]] for additional evidence that CBE's transparency and flexibility particularly benefit students from marginalized backgrounds who may experience heightened stereotype threat.
