# Peer Tutoring in Math

**Peer tutoring in math** refers to instructional arrangements where students teach or practice with other students rather than (or in addition to) working with a teacher or digital tool alone. Two primary models emerge from the research: **cross-age tutoring** (older students tutor younger ones) and **reciprocal peer tutoring** (same-age students take turns as tutor/tutee). Both have been validated as effective for [[math-fact-fluency]] building and broader math skills.

## Models

### Cross-Age Peer Tutoring

Older students (e.g., high schoolers) deliver structured interventions to younger students (e.g., elementary):

- **Evidence**: [[paper-math-fact-fluency-high-schoolers-2024|Powell et al. (2024)]] — high schoolers delivered fluency interventions as effectively as professional school-based interventionists
- **Advantages**: scalable (expand intervention capacity), socially motivating (younger students may respond better to near-peer mentors), provides tutoring experience for older students
- **Requirements**: structured training for tutors, fidelity monitoring, clear intervention protocols

### Reciprocal Peer Tutoring

Same-age students alternate between tutor and tutee roles during practice sessions:

- **Evidence**: [[paper-multiplication-fact-fluency-intervention-modality-2024|Kromminga & Codding (2024)]] — reciprocal peer tutoring produced significant multiplication fluency gains equivalent to iPad flashcards
- **Advantages**: both students learn (teaching reinforces the tutor's knowledge), no external personnel needed, builds collaborative skills
- **Requirements**: structured turn-taking, clear correction procedures, manageable group sizes

## Key Findings

### Modality Equivalence

Kromminga & Codding's RCT is the critical finding for product strategy:

| Modality | Fluency Gains | Significant vs. Control |
|---|---|---|
| Reciprocal peer tutoring | Yes | Yes |
| iPad flashcards | Yes | Yes |
| Combined (peer + iPad) | Yes | Yes |
| **Between-modality difference** | **No** | **No** |

**Interpretation**: The active ingredient is [[spaced-retrieval-practice]] (repeated retrieval of facts), not the social or technological delivery mechanism. Peer tutoring and digital tools are interchangeable from a learning-outcomes perspective.

### Cross-Age Effectiveness

Powell et al.'s finding that high schoolers match professionals has two implications:

1. **Delivery flexibility** — schools aren't limited to trained professionals for fluency intervention
2. **Social dimension** — peer tutors provide something professionals can't: relatability, role modeling, and social connection

## Social Motivation Angle

While modality equivalence shows that the *learning mechanism* doesn't depend on social interaction, the **motivational mechanism** may differ:

- **Peer tutoring** offers social accountability, belonging, and role modeling — connects to [[self-determination-theory]]'s relatedness need
- **Digital tools** offer convenience, scalability, and privacy — connects to autonomy need
- **Combined approaches** could capture both learning efficacy (via retrieval practice) and motivational engagement (via social features)

This suggests a product design opportunity: build the core learning mechanism as digital retrieval practice (validated), then layer optional social features on top for motivational boost.

## Application to Teach to One

For TTO's product strategy:

### As a Core Feature

- TTO's digital practice engine delivers the validated retrieval practice mechanism
- No need for human partners to achieve fluency gains — digital delivery is sufficient
- This validates TTO's current approach of digital-first fluency practice

### As a Supplemental Feature

- Cross-age peer tutoring could be a school-level implementation option
- TTO could provide structured protocols for schools that want to pair digital practice with peer interaction
- "Older student mentor" mode could be a feature: high school TTO students tutoring middle school TTO students

### Design Implications

- The core retrieval engine should work standalone (no social dependency)
- Social features (leaderboards, peer challenges, collaborative modes) are engagement layers, not learning dependencies
- [[gamification]] can capture some of the social motivation benefits (bounded comparison, class-level leaderboards) without requiring real-time peer interaction
