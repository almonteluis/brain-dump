# Mastery Learning

Mastery learning is Benjamin Bloom's 1968 proposal that basically every traditional classroom gets backwards: instead of holding time constant (everyone gets 2 weeks on fractions) and letting achievement vary (some kids get it, some don't), you hold *achievement* constant (everyone masters fractions) and let *time* vary. Fast kids move on. Slower kids get more practice, different approaches, more support. Nobody advances until they've actually learned the thing.

Radical in 1968. Obvious in 2026 — at least in theory. In practice, most classrooms still run on seat time because the logistics of individual pacing without technology are brutal. That's the whole reason [[teach-to-one]] exists: technology finally makes Bloom's idea operationally possible.

## Why It Works

Bloom's research showed that with mastery conditions (diagnostic feedback, corrective instruction, enough time), 80% of students could achieve at the level previously reached by only the top 20%. The gap isn't ability — it's pacing. Traditional instruction moves on whether you're ready or not, and the gaps compound. Miss place value in 2nd grade and you're paying for it in 5th.

[[knowledge-tracing]] is what makes mastery learning work in production. It gives the system a running estimate of whether the kid has actually mastered the skill — not just "did they get 70% on the test" but "what's the probability they know this specific skill right now." The [[skill-graph]] maps the sequence. [[cooldown-system]] handles the spaced review after mastery. The whole TTO stack is essentially mastery learning infrastructure.

The tension: [[productive-struggle]] requires letting kids grapple with difficulty. Mastery learning, taken to the extreme, can slide into "drill until perfect" — which kills engagement and doesn't build understanding, just fluency. The trick is knowing when "good enough" is actually good enough to move on, and that's a judgment call that algorithms still struggle with.
