# Teaching

Teaching is the act of making learning happen for someone else. Sounds simple. Isn't. A teacher is simultaneously a diagnostician (what does this kid not understand?), a curriculum designer (what should I serve next?), a motivator (why should this kid keep trying?), a feedback system (was that response close enough to validate?), and — the part no algorithm replicates — a relationship builder. Kids learn more from people they trust. Period.

The [[nctm]] 8 teaching practices are a decent map of the territory: establish goals, implement tasks that promote reasoning, use representations, facilitate discourse, pose purposeful questions, build procedural fluency from conceptual understanding, support [[productive-struggle]], elicit evidence of thinking. That's 8 jobs, most of them happening simultaneously, most of them requiring real-time judgment about a specific kid in a specific moment.

## What Ed-Tech Can and Can't Do

| Teaching function | Can tech do it? | Current state |
|-------------------|----------------|---------------|
| Diagnose gaps | Partially | [[knowledge-tracing]] is good at skill-level gaps, bad at conceptual gaps |
| Sequence content | Yes | [[skill-graph]] + orchestrator |
| Give feedback | Yes, limited | [[adaptive-feedback-education]] handles correctness, not reasoning quality |
| Motivate | Barely | [[behavioral-nudges-education]], streaks, gamification — surface-level |
| Build trust | No | This is the hard one |

[[differentiated-instruction]] is what good teachers do when they're diagnosing and sequencing on the fly for 30 different kids. [[teach-to-one]] automates the diagnosis and sequencing so the teacher can focus on the parts that actually require a human — the relational, motivational, trust-building parts. That's the pitch, anyway.

The open thread: if the best teaching is relational, and algorithms can't build relationships, then the ceiling for fully automated instruction is lower than the ed-tech industry wants to admit. The design question isn't "can we replace teachers?" — it's "can we automate enough of the mechanical stuff that teachers have more bandwidth for the human stuff?"
