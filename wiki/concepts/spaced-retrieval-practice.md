# Spaced Retrieval Practice

Spaced retrieval practice is the combination of the two most replicated findings in cognitive psychology, and it's stronger than either one alone. Space your study over time (spacing effect, [[hermann-ebbinghaus]], 1885) and make yourself actively recall instead of passively re-reading (testing effect, Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Do both and you get a multiplier, not just a bonus.

Most people get half of this right. They space their studying — spread it over days instead of cramming — but they *re-read* their notes instead of testing themselves. That's the spacing effect without the testing effect. Good, not great. The testing effect alone (cramming but with self-tests) is also good, not great. Both together is where retention actually sticks.

## Retrieval vs. Review

This distinction is the whole ballgame:

- **Review** = re-exposing yourself to the material. Re-reading notes, watching a lecture again. Feels productive. Produces the illusion of competence. You recognize the material but you can't produce it.
- **Retrieval** = pulling it out of your head without looking. Flashcards, practice problems, blank-page recall. Feels harder. *Is* harder. Works dramatically better.

This is why [[spaced-repetition]] systems force you to attempt the answer before showing it. If you just flip the flashcard over, you're reviewing. If you cover the answer and try to recall, you're retrieving. The system's job is to make you retrieve.

## The [[encoding-specificity-principle]] Problem

There's a catch. Retrieval practice is strongest when the retrieval cue matches the original learning context. Flashcard-style retrieval strips context — you see "What is 7×8?" on a blank card, which is nothing like the context where you originally learned multiplication. The cue doesn't match the encoding, so the retrieval is weaker than it should be.

[[insight-recall]] is the attempt to fix this: instead of stripping context, surface the insight *in the situation that makes it relevant*. You're not retrieving "the fact about fraction addition" — you're retrieving "what I figured out last time I was stuck on a problem like this one." The cue matches the encoding. The retrieval is stronger.

The open thread: every SRS system treats the spacing interval as the main variable — *when* to review. [[cooldown-system]] does this too. But what if the bigger lever is *what context the kid is in when the review hits*? Same skill, different context, potentially very different learning outcome. That's where [[project-atlas]] could diverge from the standard SRS playbook.
