# Differentiated Instruction

Differentiated instruction is what happens when a teacher looks at a room of 28 kids spanning three grade levels of math readiness and says: "I need a system for this." [[tomlinson]] (Carol Ann Tomlinson) built that system — not a software system, a teaching framework. Adjust content, process, product, and environment based on where each kid actually is. Not tracking (permanent sorting), not individualization (a separate plan per kid). Flexible grouping that responds to what students need *right now*.

Picture a 5th grade math class. Half the kids can multiply fractions. A quarter are still shaky on equivalent fractions. Three kids haven't mastered basic multiplication. In a traditional model, the teacher teaches to the middle and hopes for the best. ==In a differentiated classroom, those three groups get different entry points into the same overarching lesson== — maybe the struggling group works with manipulatives on fraction concepts while the ready group tackles word problems. Same destination, different on-ramps.

## The Four Vectors

Tomlinson says you can differentiate along four dimensions:

| What you change | Example | What it responds to |
|----------------|---------|-------------------|
| **Content** | Tiered texts at different reading levels | Readiness |
| **Process** | Varied activity structures | Learning profile |
| **Product** | Choice of how to demonstrate mastery | Interest |
| **Environment** | Flexible seating, quiet zones | All three |

And three student variables to differentiate *for*: readiness (current skill level → maps to [[zone-of-proximal-development]]), interest (what motivates them), and learning profile (how they take in information).

## The AI Version

Here's the thing: [[teach-to-one]] is essentially automated differentiated instruction at scale. The [[skill-graph]] handles content differentiation. The playlist handles process differentiation. [[knowledge-tracing]] estimates readiness. What Tomlinson did with teacher judgment and flexible grouping, TTO does with algorithms.

But here's the gap Tomlinson would flag: her framework includes *interest* and *learning profile*, and those are the two dimensions that are hardest to automate. A teacher who knows that Marcus loves basketball can weave that into a word problem. An algorithm that knows Marcus is at skill level 4.2 doesn't have that relational intelligence. [[ladson-billings]] would push further — culturally responsive differentiation isn't just about matching interest, it's about seeing the student's cultural context as an asset, not a variable to accommodate.

[[project-atlas]] is the attempt to close that gap — multimodal delivery, engagement optimization, maybe eventually enough signal to respond to interest and learning profile, not just readiness. But the open question is whether algorithmic differentiation can ever capture what Tomlinson calls the "knowing the learner" piece, or whether that stays permanently human.
