# Lev Vygotsky

Lev Semyonovich Vygotsky (1896–1934) was a Soviet psychologist whose work on cognitive development and social learning has become foundational to educational theory. He died at 37. Most of his influential work was published posthumously. His key concepts — the zone of proximal development, social constructivism, the role of language in thought — underpin modern instructional design.

## Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)

The ZPD is the gap between what a learner can do independently and what they can do with guidance. Learning is most effective within this zone.

- **Below ZPD** — tasks the learner can already do alone (coasting, no growth)
- **Within ZPD** — tasks achievable with support (productive struggle, optimal learning)
- **Above ZPD** — tasks beyond reach even with support (frustration, flailing)

[[scaffolding]] — temporary support that operates within the ZPD — is the direct descendant of this concept.

## Social Constructivism

Vygotsky argued that learning is fundamentally social. Knowledge is constructed through interaction with others — teachers, peers, tools. This contrasts with Piaget's emphasis on individual discovery. For Vygotsky, you don't learn alone and then join society; you learn *through* society.

## Language and Thought

Inner speech (self-talk) develops from social speech and becomes the mechanism for self-regulation and metacognition. Thinking is internalized dialogue.

## Relevance to CBL and TTO

In competency-based learning, the ZPD maps to the knowledge frontier:

- [[knowledge-tracing]] estimates mastery probability — identifying where a student sits relative to their ZPD
- [[scaffolding]] provides contingent support within the ZPD
- [[productive-struggle]] occurs when students work at the frontier of their ZPD
- The [[three-ring-mastery-system]] operationalizes ZPD-based progression

Every adaptive learning system is, at its core, a ZPD-detection machine. The algorithm's job is to keep the student in that sweet spot between coasting and drowning. Vygotsky named it in the 1930s; we're still building toward it.
