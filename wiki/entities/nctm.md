# NCTM

The National Council of Teachers of Mathematics is the closest thing US math education has to an organizing authority. They don't set policy — that's state standards and Common Core — but they set the *intellectual direction*. When NCTM says "this is good math teaching," textbooks, teacher prep programs, and assessment designers listen.

Two publications matter most:

- **Principles and Standards (2000)** — defined the NCTM Learning Principle (students must learn with understanding, not just memorize procedures) and the Equity Principle (excellence for all, not just the tracked-in kids). [[andreas-stylianides]] wrote about the Learning Principle directly.
- **Principles to Actions (2014)** — the one that put [[productive-struggle]] on the map. Listed as one of 8 Mathematics Teaching Practices: "Support productive struggle in learning mathematics." Before this, struggle was something to minimize. After this, it was something to *design for*.

## Why NCTM Matters for TTO

Every pedagogical decision in [[teach-to-one]] and [[project-atlas]] implicitly references NCTM's framework. [[master-based learning]] aligns with the Learning Principle. [[differentiated-instruction]] aligns with the Equity Principle. [[productive-struggle]] is literally one of their 8 practices. When NC pitches to districts, the NCTM alignment is part of the argument — "this isn't some tech company's theory of learning, this is what the math education community has been saying for decades, we just built the infrastructure to do it at scale."

The open tension: NCTM's practices were written for *human teachers*. "Elicit and use evidence of student thinking" is easy to say and hard to do with 30 kids. An algorithm can do it constantly, in real time, for every student — but only if "evidence of student thinking" means something a [[knowledge-tracing]] model can capture. The richness of what a skilled teacher notices in a kid's work goes beyond what current models read. Closing that gap is the [[project-atlas]] bet.
