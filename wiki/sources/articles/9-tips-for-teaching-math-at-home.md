*Justin Skycak is Chief Quant and Director of Analytics at [Math Academy](https://www.mathacademy.com/), where he designs the adaptive learning systems behind the platform. The following article draws on Justin's writing at [justinmath.com](https://justinmath.com/), his book [The Math Academy Way](https://www.justinmath.com/files/the-math-academy-way.pdf), and a recent long-form interview Justin did with Yacine Mahdid, called [The Science of Learning Math (and Anything Else)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=40hdOAOF8IU).*

The summer after tenth grade, I holed up in my room and spent eight hours a day teaching myself a year of calculus in thirty days. My mom was an art major, my dad was in healthcare logistics, and nobody in my family was mathematical, technical, scientific, or academic - so when their fifteen-year-old kept disappearing into his room to do math problems, my parents weren't quite sure what to make of it. Nobody told me to do it, and nobody really understood why I was doing it. But from the inside, it felt like leveling up in a video game. I could not stop.

During high school, I put roughly 3,000 hours into self-studying college-level math through online resources, particularly MIT OpenCourseWare and the associated textbooks. That work changed my life. It opened doors I would not have known existed. But it was also much less efficient than it could have been. I skipped review. I worked on problems that were too hard for my level. I relied too much on passive consumption at times. I discovered many learning-science principles by violating them first.

That self-study background, plus years of applying it to quantitative research and data science, plus years of tutoring and teaching, eventually led me to [Math Academy](https://www.mathacademy.com/), where I built the adaptive learning system behind the platform alongside our founder, Jason Roberts. It is also where I learned an uncomfortable lesson: most of my 3,000 self-study hours were underpowered. I hit my head on basically every ledge there was, and a much smaller number of hours, with the right structure, would have gotten me to the same place.

These are the nine tips I wish someone had handed me before I started. The learning principles behind these tips show up repeatedly throughout the research literature in the science of learning, and they are what I have seen work reliably in math education, whether the tool is Math Academy or a stack of used textbooks from Amazon.

## 1\. Do the problems. Consuming information is not enough.

I figured this one out the hard way during that first summer of self-studying calculus. After a couple weeks of grinding through problem sets, I decided I had found a hack. I spent an entire day just reading, watching videos, and skipping the exercises, feeling very efficient about how much "content" I was covering. All the information seemed to make sense when I was consuming it.

But the next morning, I sat down to do some problems and realized I had retained almost nothing. Moreover, the problems drew my attention to important details that I had completely glossed over. I thought I was internalizing information while consuming it, but it turned out I was only paying attention to the broad strokes, and even those were forgotten by the next day.

Watching a video feels like learning because the ideas are passing through your eyes and ears and your brain recognizes them as familiar enough to follow along. But recognition is not recall. Recall is what happens when you have to perform the technique yourself with a blank page in front of you. That is what matters on homework, tests, the SAT, and real problem-solving.

Learning is not the feeling of ideas making sense while they are being supplied from the outside. Learning is being able to come back later, pull those ideas back out of your own long-term memory, and use them. Comfort while consuming information is not a proxy for this, because it does not require you to pull from long-term memory. While you are reading or watching, the book or video is doing most of the lifting, so the next sentence or next step feels obvious. But that does not mean the idea has been retained in long-term memory, and it does not mean you are making progress towards building that retention. Repeatedly consuming information does far less to help you retain it, compared to repeatedly retrieving it from your own brain.

The takeaway: If your kid is watching something and nodding along, the most important part has not happened yet. The learning happens when they retrieve the idea from memory, use it to solve a problem, and get feedback. Treat the video as the warm-up, and the problem set as the workout.

## 2\. Treat confusion as diagnostic information.

A lot of parents respond to confusion by jumping in immediately: "My daughter was stuck, so I sat down and walked her through it." That is sometimes the right move and sometimes the exact wrong move.

==The key question is: how deep is the confusion?==

If the student knows the prerequisites and is stuck on a step just beyond what they already know, give them room to think. Draw their attention to explanations and examples they may have skimmed. Have them explain what they tried. Wait a little longer than feels natural. Challenging the edge of your ability is part of learning, and a kid who gets used to giving up and being rescued at the slightest sign of confusion never gets good at anything hard.

But if the student is missing the foundation underneath the problem, struggle is not productive, and the fix is not to push harder. Instead, back up and plug the hole. When a student faces extended confusion even after reading explanations carefully, working through examples, and writing out all the steps neatly on paper, the confusion often indicates that there is a missing prerequisite somewhere underneath the current topic.

The parent's job is not to rescue every time or to force the kid to suffer through every obstacle. The job is to diagnose. If the problem is one step beyond the kid's comfort level, then give them space to push through, and guide them if they're still stuck after a legitimate attempt. If the problem is out of their depth and they're drowning, then pull them back to repair their foundation.

## 3\. Master the prerequisites.

If you put "Tip 2: Treat confusion as diagnostic information" into practice, you'll quickly realize that the most painless and efficient way to deal with gaps in your student's foundation is simply to prevent them from happening in the first place.

That approach is called "mastery learning," and it's known to be one of the most reliable, largest-effect-size techniques for improving learning outcomes. In a mastery learning system, a student demonstrates that the prerequisite is actually usable before being asked to learn something that depends on it. Done well, this prevents a lot of "why can't you get this?" investigations, because the student is not being pushed into a topic while the floor underneath it is missing.

At home, you can apply the same idea. Before asking your kid to solve problems, ask: What smaller skills does this problem assume? Can my kid do those skills cleanly, without hints? If not, those smaller skills are the lesson for today.

Mastery learning is not about slowing kids down; it is about keeping them moving at the highest speed their foundations can actually support.

## 4\. Automaticity unlocks deeper understanding.

This is the one I get the most pushback on from well-meaning educators, so I want to be clear about what I mean.

Think of a basketball player who is running, dribbling, and strategizing all at the same time. If they had to consciously think about every bounce and every stride, they'd be too overwhelmed to look around and strategize. Being automatic, or having "automaticity" on foundational skills frees up attention for higher thinking. Your attention is limited, and the more that's spent on low-level mechanics, the less is left for high-level conceptualization.

The same goes for math. For instance, solving equations feels smooth when basic arithmetic is automatic - it's like moving puzzle pieces around, and you just need to identify how they fit together. But without automaticity on basic arithmetic, each puzzle piece is a heavy weight. You struggle to move them at all, much less figure out where they're supposed to go. If the mechanical moves of a subject are not fluent, the higher-level thinking has nothing to connect to and no room to happen.

The same phenomenon occurs throughout all levels of math. Automaticity on arithmetic unlocks algebra. Automaticity on algebra unlocks calculus. Automaticity on calculus unlocks even higher-level math and engineering applications.

Here's a concrete example from a class I used to teach. I had advanced high school students working on vanishing and exploding gradients in neural networks, which is a topic that university students and even machine learning professionals often have a very shallow understanding of. Instead of jumping right into the concept, I had them work out the computations for a tiny neural network by hand. After several minutes of chain-ruling their way through the calculus, they could see the derivative either blowing up or shrinking to nothing depending on the choice of activation function. At that point, the concept not only landed immediately, but they could also infer what other activation functions would lead to vanishing or exploding gradients because they understood how the math would play out.

Students who only "understand" the idea abstractly, without having worked through the math themself, never really get there - and students who don't have automaticity on their foundational mathematics can't work through the math themselves.

The homeschool translation is simple: Practice the basic moves until they are fast, accurate, and low-effort. Do not skip this part because it feels mechanical. The mechanical part is what makes deeper understanding possible.

## 5\. Treat math like training, not cramming.

If you wanted your kid to get good at basketball, you would not expect one ninety-minute practice per week to do the job. You would expect short daily reps, a coach watching them move, corrections on the fly, and years of cumulative practice on fundamentals that never go away. Every parent who has ever played a sport seriously, or whose kid has played a sport seriously, understands this in their bones.

Math is much closer to athletics than most people realize. Just like any sport, math is a skill hierarchy. If arithmetic is shaky, algebra is harder. If algebra is shaky, calculus is harder. The fundamentals never go away, and the only way to move beyond them is to practice regularly.

Short, focused study sessions done frequently will beat the occasional marathon. The student who practices regularly gets more total reps, more frequent feedback, and less time for previous knowledge to decay. The student who waits for one giant weekly session spends too much of that session warming back up, never getting to the point where the basics feel automatic, and never getting to the interesting stuff.

## 6\. Space out exercises of the same kind.

Besides simply getting more reps, there is a deeper reason why short, frequent training sessions work so well: spacing increases the value of each rep.

If your kid does thirty of the same kind of problem in one sitting, the first few may require real thinking, but by the end they will be running on short-term momentum: the method is still warm in their temporary working memory, which means they're not being required to pull it from long-term memory.

It's the same problem as thinking you're learning while repeatedly consuming information: you're not being made to pull the information from long-term memory, so most of what you think you're retaining is actually passing you by.

So how do you ensure that later problems still require you to pull from long-term memory, as opposed to running on short-term momentum?

The trick is to let time pass. A day later, the memory is a little fuzzier. Pulling it back up takes more effort, and that is exactly why the rep is valuable. Successful retrieval after a delay does not just restore the memory; it helps consolidate it even deeper into long-term storage and slows down future forgetting. Same clock time, different learning value.

This is known in the research literature as the "spacing effect," and - to quote researcher Doug Rohrer - it is "one of the largest and most robust findings in learning research, and it appears to have few constraints."

The takeaway: If you space out the exercises of the same kind - say, five today, five tomorrow, five a few days later, five the following week, five a couple weeks later, and five a couple months later - you'll get much more learning per exercise as compared to doing all thirty exercises in one sitting. Spaced practice gives the brain time to forget just enough that remembering becomes a productive rep. That is why regular practice beats cramming even when the total volume of work is identical.

## 7\. Most "motivation problems" are actually sequencing problems.

When a kid starts avoiding math, the reflex is to talk about motivation. Make it fun. Find what they are interested in. Gamify it. Encourage them more.

Those things can help, but they do not address the more common root cause: the student is being asked to work on something that depends on knowledge they don't have solidly in place.

The kid cannot do the problems. They know they cannot do the problems. And because "I do not know what I am doing" is hard for any kid to admit, it often comes out as "I do not want to," which looks like a motivation problem, even though it is a sequencing problem.

This is where encouragement can accidentally backfire. If a kid genuinely does not have the prerequisites, telling them "You can do it, just keep trying" may sound supportive, but from their point of view it feels like being asked to do something impossible and then blamed for not doing it. After enough of that, the kid learns a different lesson: "I am bad at math."

Encouragement works best when the task is actually within reach. If it is not within reach, the kindest thing is not a louder pep talk. It is to reduce the cognitive friction by backing up to the missing skill, getting a win there, and rebuilding the path forward.

Once the sequence lines up again, the "motivation problem" often disappears on its own. Nothing succeeds like success, and once a kid starts solving problems correctly and confidently again, the engine restarts.

This is why prerequisite structure matters so much. A good learning system should always be considering what the student knows, what they are ready to learn next, and whether any foundations need repair.

## 8\. Review like you mean it.

One of the biggest differences between how real learning works and how most home math routines end up going is review. Your kid learns long division in March. By June it's as if they've never seen it before. This is not because they are bad at math - it is because knowledge decays when it is not revisited, and most curricula are built in a straight line, not in a spiral. Their knowledge was simply not revisited enough to stay usable.

The antidote is called "spaced repetition," and the idea is simple: after your kid learns a skill, bring it back right as it is starting to get fuzzy. Have them try to retrieve the method from memory and solve a problem without a refresher. If they get it, wait longer before the next review. If they miss it or need help, bring it back sooner.

For math, the review should usually be problem-based, not flashcard-based. Don't just ask, "Do you remember long division?" Instead, put a long-division problem into a mixed set next to fractions, decimals, equations, and whatever else they have learned, and replace it with a different problem each time they solve it. That mixed context matters because real mastery means recognizing which tool to use when the problem is no longer labeled for you.

The goal is not to keep every old topic equally fresh every day. The goal is to keep important skills retrievable over longer and longer intervals, while mixing them enough that your kid learns not just how to execute a method, but when to use it.

A lot of my early work at Math Academy revolved around spaced repetition in hierarchical knowledge structures, in particular developing an algorithm we call FIRe (Fractional Implicit Repetition). The core insight is that math is not a pile of isolated exercises.

Math builds upon itself, and whenever your kid solves a problem, they are implicitly reviewing all the simpler component skills that they're using to solve the problem. If they multiply two fractions by multiplying the numerators and multiplying the denominators, then they have effectively practiced multiplying whole numbers as well. If they factor a quadratic into linear factors and solve the resulting linear equations, they have effectively practiced solving linear equations too.

A good review system takes advantage of that compounding instead of treating every skill as an independent flashcard you have to keep refreshing separately on its own clock.

But not every prerequisite is practiced by every later topic. Some skills are merely background knowledge. Others are component skills that get exercised directly. When component skills are exercised, they are sometimes exercised fractionally, and other times fully. A good review system has to know the difference.

The homeschool version is simpler than the algorithm: never let old material disappear completely. Keep a running bank of mixed-review problems that covers important skills your kid has previously learned, and cycle through it. If your kid solves a review problem successfully without assistance, wait a while longer before bringing it up again. If they need a refresher, help them through and revisit it again sooner. A handful of spaced mixed-review problems at the start of each session can do wonders for long-term retention.

## 9\. The broader goal is to get good at something hard.

This last one is not strictly about math. When I was fifteen I holed up in my bedroom and worked on math problems all day for the entire summer. Nobody told me to do it, and nobody really understood why I was doing it. I did it because it felt like leveling up in a video game, and I had never experienced anything like it before in real life.

That experience changed my trajectory. Not just because I learned so much math, but also because I experienced what it felt like to choose something hard, work at it seriously, and become capable of things I could not do a month earlier. It is one of the most valuable experiences education can give a kid.

Math is a good delivery mechanism because the feedback is fast and the prerequisite structure is unusually clear. You solve the problem or you do not. You know the prerequisite or you do not. You can see yourself getting better by the day.

But the bigger prize is not any single topic. The bigger prize is the habit of serious, systematic improvement.

If you teach math at home and your kid learns to enjoy measurable progress at something genuinely hard, you have done one of the most important parts of the job. Curriculum, schedule, and review system all matter. But the deepest win is helping a kid realize that hard things can become theirs through good training and consistent work.
