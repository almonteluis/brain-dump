# Metacognitive Skills in Math Problem Solving (Tolvanen et al. 2025)

## Summary

Study examining how metacognitive skills manifest during math problem solving and how structured reflective prompts affect metacognitive engagement. Finding: math problems alone activate *some* metacognition, but structured reflective prompts **dramatically enhance** planning, monitoring, and evaluation. The most common metacognitive utterance was "creating a learning goal and a plan" (f=221). Combined goal-setting + actionable planning produced deeper engagement than superficial performance goals alone. Recommends embedding reflective prompts directly within math problems rather than as separate reflection activities.

## Key Claims

- Math problems alone activate baseline metacognition, but **structured reflective prompts dramatically enhance** all three metacognitive phases: planning, monitoring, and evaluation.
- "Creating a learning goal and a plan" was the **most frequent metacognitive utterance** (f=221), indicating that planning is the most naturally accessible metacognitive entry point.
- **Goal-setting + actionable planning** produces deeper engagement than superficial performance goals ("I want to get a good score").
- Reflective prompts should be **embedded within the math problem flow** rather than appended as separate post-problem reflection activities.
- The quality of metacognitive engagement matters — not just whether students self-monitor, but *what* they monitor and *how* they plan.

## Why This Matters for Teach to One

Teach to One presents students with varied math problems daily. This paper provides direct evidence that the *format* of those problems matters for metacognitive development. Simply presenting problems isn't enough — TTO should embed structured reflective prompts like "What strategy will you use?" before problem-solving and "Did your approach work?" after completion. The finding that goal-setting + actionable planning outperforms performance goals has direct implications for [[gamification]] design: motivating students with mastery-oriented goals (not just score/completion) may activate deeper metacognition. This also connects to [[productive-struggle]] — prompts that help students plan and monitor during struggle may convert unproductive frustration into productive learning.
