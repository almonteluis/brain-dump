# Spaced Retrieval in Calculus: Desirable Difficulty (Lyle et al., 2022)

## Summary

Lyle, Bego, Ralston, and Immekus (2022) investigated whether spaced retrieval practice would improve learning in a college calculus course. Using a within-subjects design, they found that students in the spaced condition performed **significantly worse on practice quizzes** but **significantly better on the final exam** compared to the massed condition. This pattern is the hallmark of Robert Bjork's [[desirable-difficulty]] framework: short-term performance costs are outweighed by durable long-term retention gains.

## Key Claims

1. **Spacing impaired quiz performance but improved final exam scores** — the classic desirable difficulty tradeoff was replicated in a procedural/conceptual math context
2. **Spacing works beyond memorization** — calculus involves procedural knowledge and conceptual understanding, not just factual recall, yet spacing still produced robust retention benefits
3. **The short-term cost is real and predictable** — students will feel like they're performing worse during spaced practice, which poses a motivation/engagement risk for adaptive learning platforms
4. **Consistent with prior work** — extends earlier findings from precalculus (Lyle et al., 2020; Hopkins et al., 2016) to calculus
5. **Feedback is an enabling condition** — all cited spaced retrieval studies in math classrooms provided feedback to students
