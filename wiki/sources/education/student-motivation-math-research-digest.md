# Student Motivation for Mathematics — Research Digest (Middle & High School)

Compiled from ERIC, Semantic Scholar, and arXiv searches. Focus: grades 6-12, ages 11-18.

---

## A. Key Theoretical Frameworks

### 1. Self-Determination Theory (SDT)
Ryan & Deci's framework posits three basic psychological needs: autonomy, competence, and relatedness. When these are satisfied, intrinsic motivation flourishes.

**Key findings from the research:**
- Valas & Sovik (1993) — Longitudinal study of 161 7th graders and 164 8th graders showed that controlling teacher strategies negatively impact student achievement, interest, and math self-concept. When teachers supported autonomy, students showed stronger intrinsic motivation.
- Shank, Tang & Morris (2025) — Applied SDT to online secondary math course design. Found that supporting self-regulation and self-efficacy are critical for online math learners who struggle more without in-person structure.
- Irvine (2019) — Used SDT as framework for preservice math teacher courses, emphasizing that teacher candidates need to understand motivation theory to design effective instruction.
- Corkin, Ekmekci & Parr (2018) — Found that school-work environment factors predict math teachers' own self-efficacy and intrinsic value for teaching, which cascades to student motivation.

### 2. Expectancy-Value Theory (EVT)
Eccles et al.'s theory: motivation = expectancy of success × subjective task value (intrinsic value, utility value, attainment value, cost).

**Key findings:**
- Trautwein, Marsh & Nagengast (2012) — Found that expectancy and value beliefs additively (not multiplicatively) predict performance, persistence, and task choice in modern formulations of EVT.
- Safavian & Conley (2016) — Studied 926 7th-grade prealgebra students (76% Hispanic, 76% low income). EVT beliefs predicted both math achievement and enrollment decisions. Utility value was especially predictive for underrepresented students.
- Kang & Leung (2023) — Demonstrated measurement invariance of expectancy-value constructs across two ethnic cultures, suggesting the framework applies broadly.
- Ozulku & Kloser (2024) — Found middle school students' math motivational dispositions (expectancy + value) predict STEM career interest.

### 3. Achievement Goal Theory
Students pursue different types of goals: mastery (learning-oriented) vs. performance (appearance-oriented), with approach vs. avoidance variants.

**Key findings:**
- Deevers (2010) — Longitudinal study of 800 students (2006-2009) found teacher goal endorsement predicts student achievement goals and math achievement on standardized tests.
- Gonzalez & Wolters (2006) — Examined both achievement goal theory AND self-determination theory in Algebra I students. Found parenting styles and involvement predict student motivation through both theoretical lenses.
- Burns, Martin & Evans (2021) — Teacher feedback + feedforward combined with personal best goal setting improved math achievement. Feedback that guides improvement (not just corrects errors) was key.

### 4. Growth Mindset (Dweck)
Belief that intelligence is malleable vs. fixed. Interventions aim to shift students toward growth mindset.

**Key findings:**
- Balan & Sjöwall (2023) — Evaluated deliberate practice + growth mindset intervention in 7th-grade math. Previous studies showed small effect sizes; this study found limited additional benefit of mindset training beyond deliberate practice alone.
- Song, Jiang & Ji (2022) — Growth mindset training weakened the effect of math-gender stereotype threat on 11th-grade girls. Mindset moderated the stereotype threat effect.
- Curry & Chan (2025) — Online growth mindset intervention improved perceived flexibility of intelligence in community college corequisite math courses.
- Chupp (2021) — Elementary growth mindset interventions increased growth mindset beliefs but effects on math achievement were mixed.

### 5. Math Anxiety
A specific anxiety domain with cognitive (worry, intrusive thoughts) and affective (fear, dread) components that directly impair math performance.

**Key findings:**
- Pizzie & Kraemer (2023) — Reviewed strategies for remediating math anxiety in high school. Math anxiety is associated with maladaptive study behaviors, avoidance, and reduced working memory capacity. Evidence supports expressive writing, cognitive reappraisal, and anxiety-reducing test formats.
- Schmitz, Jansen, Wiers & Salemink (2023) — Found three cognitive biases in math-anxious adolescents: math-failure associations, attentional biases, and avoidance bias. These biases reinforce each other in a cycle.
- Wang & Wei (2025) — Gen-AI assisted learning reduced math anxiety in primary students. The personalized, non-judgmental nature of AI tutoring helped reduce fear of negative evaluation.
- Altakhayneh (2020) — Math anxiety directly and negatively impacts middle school achievement in Amman, Jordan. Gender differences were found (girls reported higher anxiety).
- Scrivner (2024) — Mastery-based learning reduced math anxiety in middle level students by removing time pressure and allowing multiple attempts.

## B. Gamification & Game-Based Learning in Math

### High-impact papers from Semantic Scholar:

1. **Hossein-Mohand et al. (2021)** — "Analysis of the Use and Integration of the Flipped Learning Model, Project-Based Learning, and Gamification Methodologies by Secondary School Mathematics Teachers" — 76 citations. Found that combining gamification with flipped learning and PBL produced the strongest engagement and achievement outcomes.

2. **Malvasi, Gil-Quintana & Bocciolesi (2022)** — "The Projection of Gamification and Serious Games in the Learning of Mathematics" — 19 citations. Multi-case study of Italian secondary schools. Chess as a serious game improved both engagement and mathematical reasoning.

3. **Parrales et al. (2024)** — "Gamification for learning mathematics in secondary school: Most effective gamified strategies." Identified the most effective strategies grounded in flow theory. Key elements: adaptive difficulty, immediate feedback, narrative framing, and social competition.

4. **Origenes et al. (2025)** — Meta-analysis of gamification effects on math learning. Found moderate positive effects on motivation (d=0.52) and smaller but significant effects on achievement (d=0.34). Effects were stronger for lower-performing students.

5. **Susilawati et al. (2025)** — "Integrating realistic mathematics education, AI, and gamification." Found that combining real-world context (RME), AI personalization, and gamification elements fostered both motivation and problem-solving skills.

### From ERIC:

6. **Lo & Hew (2020)** — Grade 9 comparison: flipped learning with gamification outperformed traditional learning and online independent study on both math achievement AND cognitive engagement.

7. **Vanacore, Sales, Liu et al. (2023)** — Gamification benefits "persistent learners" most — students who replay problems gain more from gamified platforms. Propensity to replay moderates effectiveness.

8. **Bryant (2024)** — Gamification + standards-based grading in secondary math improved student perceptions of engagement. Removing traditional grading reduced anxiety and increased willingness to attempt challenging problems.

9. **Algburi, Almekhlafi & Jarrah (2026)** — Gamification improved 6th-grade math performance and motivation in UAE, addressing PISA-identified challenges.

## C. Math Anxiety Interventions

| Strategy | Evidence | Source |
|----------|----------|--------|
| Expressive writing before tests | Reduces anxiety-related performance decrements by offloading worry | Pizzie & Kraemer (2023) |
| Cognitive reappraisal | Reframing anxiety as activation rather than threat | Pizzie & Kraemer (2023) |
| Mastery-based learning | Removes time pressure, allows multiple attempts | Scrivner (2024) |
| Creative teaching strategies | Multiple strategies identified by middle/high school educators | Grzybowski (2022) |
| Growth mindset training | Weakens stereotype threat, especially for girls | Song, Jiang & Ji (2022) |
| AI-assisted learning | Non-judgmental, personalized feedback reduces evaluation fear | Wang & Wei (2025) |
| Mindfulness interventions | 10-week school-based program reduced depression/anxiety symptoms | Sandberg et al. (2024) |

## D. Math Identity & Belonging

- **Darragh (2013)** — Students constructing math identity at the transition to secondary school. Found that girls' confidence and belonging in math are especially fragile during this transition period.
- **Legette & Kurtz-Costes (2021)** — Curricular tracking in 6th grade negatively affects school belonging for non-honors students, which cascades to reduced math identity and motivation.
- **Bondurant & McConchie (2024)** — "Portrait of a Math Person" — math identity factors span four categories: (1) past math experiences, (2) self-perception as a math person, (3) social recognition, and (4) future utility.

## E. Key Observation: Motivation Decline Across Grades

- **DiCintio & Stevens (1997)** — Found that 6th and 7th grade math lessons had significantly fewer higher-order thinking elements than 5th grade, and these students reported much less intrinsic motivation than 5th graders. Motivation drops sharply at the middle school transition.
- **Herges, Duffield, Martin & Wageman (2017)** — Surveyed 65 middle school math students. Found significant positive correlations between intrinsic motivation and self-reported math grades, enjoyment, and confidence. High vs. low achievers differed significantly on intrinsic motivation, math value, enjoyment, confidence, and parental involvement. No gender differences found.
- **Ning (2020)** — PISA Shanghai data (N=5,177): orderly school disciplinary climate helps achievement but can hurt instrumental motivation. Trade-off between performance and motivation.

## F. Spaced/Retrieval Practice in Math

- **Hopkins, Lyle & Hieb (2016)** — Spaced retrieval practice increased both short-term and long-term retention of college math knowledge. Particularly effective for cumulative disciplines like mathematics.
- Note: Most retrieval practice research is in higher education; gaps remain for middle/high school application.

## G. Open Questions & Gaps

1. **Long-term effects** — Most gamification and mindset interventions are short-term (weeks). Long-term durability is largely unknown.
2. **Individual differences** — Vanacore et al. (2023) showed gamification works best for persistent learners. Who else benefits or is left behind?
3. **AI-assisted motivation** — Wang & Wei (2025) found AI reduced math anxiety, but this is very new territory. How does AI tutoring affect intrinsic vs. extrinsic motivation?
4. **Equity** — Safavian & Conley (2016) found utility value especially predicts enrollment for underrepresented students. Can app-based interventions deliver this?
5. **Middle school transition** — The sharp motivation decline at grade 6 is well-documented but poorly addressed by interventions.
6. **Gamification quality** — Not all gamification is equal. Parrales et al. (2024) identified specific effective strategies, but many implementations use superficial points/badges without deeper design.
